# Vietnamese → English Interference Notes — Restaurant & Café Domain

**Author:** A5 · **Scope:** the restaurant and café service-interaction layer —
`gọi món` (ordering in depth), `thanh toán` (paying and billing), `khẩu vị`
(taste, flavour, and preference vocabulary) — for both dine-in and café settings ·
**Audience:** Vietnamese adults eating out in English-speaking environments:
diaspora learners navigating North American/Australian/UK restaurants and cafés,
overseas students on dates and group outings, and IELTS/TOEIC candidates who need
functional spoken English beyond the classroom.

Tenth in the series (after `interference-notes-small-talk.md`,
`interference-notes-shopping-services.md`, `interference-notes-health-pharmacy.md`,
and others). This file is a companion to `interference-notes-food-cooking.md`, which
covers count/mass nouns, ordering frames (`I'll have / Can I get`), modifier stacking,
allergies, complaints, and basic tipping. That file is the grammar and ordering
foundation; **this file deepens three sub-domains the foundation file only touches**:
(1) `khẩu vị` taste and flavour vocabulary, (2) the café-specific ordering register,
(3) the full restaurant-service arc from reservation to leaving.

Same contrastive format: **VN** = Vietnamese source · **EN✗** = typical learner
output · **EN✓** = natural English target. MercyBlade principle: interference as
**friendly context, never a scolding**; the goal is a confident, warm adult voice —
someone who can describe their taste preferences clearly, order a specialty coffee
without hesitation, and handle a billing error without losing composure.

## 1. Why these errors happen — structural facts specific to this domain

1. **`Khẩu vị` is a one-word summary in VN.** `Khẩu vị của tôi` = "my taste/palate"
   — a single noun phrase covers food preference, spice tolerance, and dietary
   habits simultaneously. English breaks this into distinct vocabulary clusters:
   flavour words (`sweet`, `salty`, `sour`, `bitter`, `umami`), texture words
   (`crunchy`, `tender`, `silky`), preference phrases (`I prefer…`, `I tend to
   like…`), and tolerance statements (`I can't do too spicy`). Learners reach for
   a single short phrase where English expects layered specificity.

2. **VN café culture is different.** Traditional Vietnamese coffee (`cà phê sữa đá`,
   `cà phê đen`) is brewed from Robusta, poured over sweetened condensed milk. The
   third-wave English café (`flat white`, `pour-over`, `oat latte`) uses entirely
   different vocabulary. Learners who are fluent coffee drinkers in VN walk into a
   Melbourne or Seattle café with a vocabulary gap and order "black coffee" when they
   want something more specific, or freeze at the barista's upsell questions.

3. **Restaurant service culture has a different script.** Vietnamese casual dining
   is often faster, less staged, and less verbally interactive. English sit-down
   restaurant service has a predictable sequence: greeting → drinks order → food
   order → mid-meal check-in → dessert offer → bill request. Each stage has its
   own stock phrases. Learners who don't know the script get caught off-guard or
   stay silent at the wrong moments.

4. **Group dining in VN is communal; in English contexts it is individual.** VN
   dining defaults to sharing dishes (`ăn chung`). English menus are mostly
   individually portioned; sharing is explicit (`we're sharing dishes`). Learners
   may order as if the table shares, or feel uncertain asking for a shared platter.

5. **`Thanh toán` covers the whole payment event.** In VN, calling `tính tiền` or
   `thanh toán` ends the interaction. In English sit-down restaurants, the bill
   sequence has several sub-moments: requesting the bill, reading the itemized
   receipt, querying a charge, deciding on tip, choosing payment method, getting the
   receipt. Each stage uses different vocabulary.

## 2. Taste and flavour vocabulary (`khẩu vị`)

### 2.1 The five basic tastes — and the gaps

Vietnamese names the five tastes clearly: `ngọt` (sweet), `mặn` (salty/savoury),
`chua` (sour), `đắng` (bitter), `ngon/béo` (umami/rich). English has good parallels,
but several VN–EN pairs are not one-to-one:

| VN | EN✗ (direct map) | EN✓ (correct word) | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ngọt` | "sweet" ✓ | **sweet** | direct map works |
| `mặn` | "salty" | **salty** (in excess) / **savoury** (pleasant) | `mặn` is neutral; `salty` in EN often means too much salt |
| `chua` | "sour" | **sour** (strong) / **tangy** (mild, pleasant) | `tangy` covers the pleasant chua of a dressing |
| `đắng` | "bitter" ✓ | **bitter** | direct map works |
| `cay` | "spicy" ✓ | **spicy** / **hot** | both work; `hot` = temperature OR spice — clarify if needed |
| `béo` / `ngậy` | "fatty" | **rich** / **creamy** / **buttery** | `fatty` sounds negative; `rich` is positive |
| `ngon` | "delicious / good" | **delicious** / **tasty** / **flavourful** | `ngon` = both "tastes good" and `flavourful`; pick the right one |
| `nhạt` | "bland" / "pale" | **mild** (if neutral) / **bland** (negative) | `nhạt` is neutral in VN; `bland` is a complaint in EN |
| `umami` | — | **savoury** / **umami** / **rich** / **meaty** | no VN word; describe with texture + taste combos |

- VN: *Tôi thích vị chua nhẹ.* → EN✗ "I like a little sour taste." → EN✓ **"I like
  a mild tanginess."** / **"I prefer things on the tangier side."**
- VN: *Tôi không ăn được cay.* → EN✗ "I can't eat spicy." → EN✓ **"I can't handle
  spicy food."** / **"I'm not great with heat."**
- VN: *Món này béo quá.* → EN✗ "This dish is too fatty." → EN✓ **"This is a bit too
  rich for me."** (`fatty` is technically correct but sounds critical; `rich` is
  the standard positive/neutral adjective for a creamy or butter-heavy dish)

### 2.2 Describing spice level

Spice level is one of the most frequent khẩu vị conversations in a restaurant; it
also has a standard vocabulary that learners often don't know:

| VN spice level | EN✗ | EN✓ |
|---|---|---|
| `không cay` | "no spicy" | **"not spicy, please"** / **"mild, please"** |
| `ít cay` | "less spicy" | **"mild"** / **"on the milder side"** / **"not too hot"** |
| `vừa cay` | "medium spicy" | **"medium heat"** / **"medium spice"** |
| `cay vừa vừa` | "a little spicy" | **"just a little heat"** / **"slightly spicy"** |
| `cay thật sự` | "very spicy" / "a lot spicy" | **"extra spicy"** / **"as spicy as you can make it"** |

- At a Thai restaurant: **"Could I get it mild?"** / **"How spicy is the Pad Thai?"**
  / **"Can you make it medium heat?"**
- Teaching note: restaurants often use a 1–5 scale or ask `mild / medium / hot /
  extra hot`. Knowing `mild` and `medium` as the two most useful anchors covers most
  situations.

### 2.3 Texture vocabulary — the hidden second layer of `khẩu vị`

Texture words are rarely taught but come up constantly when describing food preferences:

| Texture | VN equivalent | When it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **crunchy** | giòn | fried items, toast, vegetables |
| **crispy** | giòn rụm | fried skin, chips, crackers (thin items) |
| **tender** | mềm / non | meat cooked until soft |
| **chewy** | dai | boba, certain meats, overcooked pasta |
| **silky** | mượt / mịn | tofu, custard, smooth sauces |
| **flaky** | xốp / bở | fish, pastry |
| **gooey** | sền sệt / dính | melted cheese, brownies |
| **mushy** | nhũn | overcooked vegetables, complaint word |
| **al dente** | — | pasta cooked firm; Italian term but standard in EN restaurants |

- VN: *Tôi thích thịt mềm, không muốn dai.* → EN✗ "I like soft meat, not hard." →
  EN✓ **"I prefer tender meat — not too chewy."**
- VN: *Cái này bị nhũn.* → EN✗ "This is soft soft." → EN✓ **"This is a bit mushy —
  is it meant to be like this?"**
- Teaching note: `mushy` is almost always a complaint; `soft` and `tender` are
  compliments. Switching the adjective changes the social register of the sentence.

### 2.4 Doneness for meat and eggs

Vietnamese cooking styles for beef and eggs do not map onto English steak/egg
doneness vocabulary. This is a high-frequency ordering gap in Western restaurants:

**Steak doneness:**

| EN term | Description | VN approximate |
|---|---|---|
| **rare** | red, warm centre | tái, còn đỏ bên trong |
| **medium rare** | pink, slightly warm centre | tái ít, hơi hồng |
| **medium** | pink centre, mostly cooked | vừa, hơi hồng |
| **medium well** | slightly pink | gần chín, ít hồng |
| **well-done** | fully cooked, no pink | chín hẳn |

- EN✗ (when asked "How would you like it?") "Normal, okay, any way." → EN✓ **"Medium
  rare, please."** / **"I'd like it medium."**
- Teaching note: `well-done` is a completely legitimate preference; it will never
  offend a server. If unsure, **"medium"** is a safe neutral that most people enjoy.

**Egg style:**

| EN term | Description | VN approximate |
|---|---|---|
| **scrambled** | trứng bác / trứng khuấy | beaten + fried until fluffy |
| **fried** (sunny-side up) | trứng ốp la một mặt | yolk up, unbroken |
| **over easy** | trứng ốp la hai mặt, lòng đỏ chảy | flipped, runny yolk |
| **over hard** | trứng ốp la chín hẳn | flipped, solid yolk |
| **poached** | trứng chần | dropped in simmering water |
| **boiled** | trứng luộc | soft-boiled (5–7 min) or hard-boiled (10+ min) |

- EN✗ "I want egg fried, the one with yellow on top." → EN✓ **"Can I get a
  fried egg? Sunny-side up."**
- EN✗ "Scramble egg please." → EN✓ **"Scrambled eggs, please."** (plural — eggs
  is almost always plural for a portion)

## 3. Café-specific ordering

### 3.1 Core coffee vocabulary

English café menus assume knowledge of Italian-derived espresso terms. VN learners
who are fluent coffee drinkers feel confused in a Western café:

| Drink | What it is | VN nearest parallel |
|---|---|---|
| **espresso** | 30ml concentrated shot, no milk | cà phê phin đặc |
| **americano** | espresso + hot water | cà phê đen pha loãng |
| **latte** | espresso + steamed milk (lots) | cà phê sữa nóng (closest) |
| **cappuccino** | espresso + steamed milk + foam | cà phê bọt sữa |
| **flat white** | espresso + microfoam (less milk than latte) | — (no VN parallel) |
| **macchiato** | espresso + a dot of foam or milk | cà phê có chút sữa |
| **cold brew** | coffee steeped cold overnight | — (different from cà phê đá) |
| **pour-over** | drip filter, slow | gần giống phin nhưng khác |
| **iced latte** | latte over ice | cà phê sữa đá |

- Teaching note: **`latte`** without any qualifier is hot by default. For cold:
  **`iced latte`**. For non-dairy: **`oat latte`** / **`almond latte`** / **`soy latte`**.
- Asking for help is socially normal: **"I'm not sure what to get — what's the
  difference between a latte and a flat white?"** Baristas are used to explaining.

### 3.2 Customisation at a café counter

The third-wave café has many options that VN learners may not know to ask about:

| Option | EN✓ phrase | VN gloss |
|---|---|---|
| Milk alternative | "Can I get **oat milk**?" / "with **almond milk**?" | sữa yến mạch / sữa hạnh nhân |
| Extra shot | "Can I get an **extra shot**?" | thêm một shot cà phê |
| Decaf | "Can I get that **decaf**?" / "**decaffeinated**, please" | không caffeine |
| Syrup flavour | "Can I add **vanilla syrup**?" | si-rô vani |
| Sugar-free syrup | "**sugar-free vanilla**, please" | si-rô không đường |
| Temperature | "**a bit cooler**, please" / "**not too hot**" | bớt nóng |
| Size | "**small / medium / large**" or "**tall / grande / venti**" (Starbucks) | nhỏ / vừa / lớn |
| Strength | "**strong**, please" / "**extra shot**" | đậm / mạnh |

- EN✗ "Give me coffee no milk, medium size, not sweet." → EN✓ **"Can I get a medium
  Americano? No milk, and no sugar, please."**
- Teaching note: the barista's `"For here or to go?"` is the standard café opener
  in North America. Answer: **"For here"** (dine in) / **"To go"** / **"Take away"**
  (UK/AUS). One or two words is all that's needed.

### 3.3 The barista's upsell questions

Several questions catch learners off guard:

| Barista asks | VN learner often | EN✓ response |
|---|---|---|
| "What size?" | "Medium." (fine but hesitant) | **"Medium, please."** |
| "Any milk or sugar?" | "No…" (trails off) | **"Black, please."** / **"Just milk, no sugar."** |
| "Room for milk?" | (confused) | **"No, fill it up, please."** / **"Yes, leave a little room."** |
| "Name for the order?" | "How to spell?" | Just say your name: **"Linh."** |
| "Would you like anything else?" | (silent / look away) | **"No thanks, that's all."** |

- Teaching note: **"Room for milk?"** = `Để khoảng trống cho sữa không?` — the
  barista is asking if you'll be adding milk yourself (at the condiment bar) so they
  won't fill the cup to the brim.

## 4. The restaurant service arc

### 4.1 Making and managing a reservation

| VN intent | EN✗ | EN✓ |
|---|---|---|
| Đặt bàn | "I want reserve table." | **"I'd like to make a reservation."** |
| Cho 4 người | "We are 4 people." | **"A table for four, please."** |
| 7 giờ tối | "7 o'clock night." | **"At 7 pm."** |
| Tên | "My name is Linh." ✓ | **"The reservation is under Linh."** (at the door) |
| Thay đổi | "I want change time." | **"I need to change my reservation."** |
| Hủy | "I want cancel." | **"I need to cancel my reservation."** |
| Đến trễ | "I will late 10 minute." | **"We'll be about 10 minutes late."** |

- EN✓ Phone reservation: **"Hi, I'd like to make a reservation for four people
  this Saturday at 7 pm. The name is Linh."**
- EN✓ Arriving: **"Hi, I have a reservation under the name Linh."**

### 4.2 Waiting and being seated

| Situation | EN✗ | EN✓ |
|---|---|---|
| Hỏi thời gian chờ | "How long I wait?" | **"How long is the wait?"** / **"How long is the wait for a table of four?"** |
| Chưa sẵn sàng ngồi | "We wait outside." | **"We'll wait at the bar / outside."** |
| Xác nhận đã đặt | "I have reserve." | **"I have a reservation — under Linh, 7 pm."** |
| Yêu cầu bàn cụ thể | "I want window seat." | **"Could we get a table by the window, if possible?"** |
| Ghế cao cho trẻ | "I need high chair baby." | **"Could we get a high chair, please?"** |

- Teaching note: if there's no reservation and the wait is long, **"Is there a
  wait list?"** or **"Can you put us on the wait list?"** are the stock phrases.

### 4.3 The greeting-and-drinks stage

After being seated, the server's first line is almost always:
- **"Can I start you off with some drinks?"** / **"Can I get you something to
  drink?"** / **"Would you like to hear about our specials today?"**

VN learners who aren't expecting this opener often say nothing or agree to
everything. Useful responses:

- **"Just water for now, thanks."** — buys time while looking at the menu.
- **"Could we have a few minutes to look at the drinks menu?"**
- **"We'll start with two sparkling waters."**
- Teaching note: asking for `tap water` is free and culturally normal in most
  English-speaking restaurants — **"Just tap water, please."** Saying `a water`
  works too; the server knows what you mean.

### 4.4 Mid-meal check-in — "Is everything alright?"

(Covered in `interference-notes-food-cooking.md` §7.3 as a complaint trigger;
this note covers the **positive use** of the same opener.)

The server's mid-meal check-in is also the window to make requests:
- **"Yes, everything's great — actually, could we get some extra napkins?"**
- **"It's delicious, thanks! Could we get some more bread?"**
- **"We're fine, but could we get another bottle of sparkling water when you
  have a chance?"**

The phrase `when you have a chance` = signal that you're not urgent, the server
can come back. Very common and appreciated in busy restaurants.

### 4.5 Requesting things during the meal

| VN request | EN✗ | EN✓ |
|---|---|---|
| Thêm nước | "More water." | **"Could we get some more water?"** / **"Another round of waters when you have a chance?"** |
| Thêm khăn ăn | "Give me napkins." | **"Could we have some extra napkins, please?"** |
| Thêm bánh mì | "More bread." | **"Could we get some more bread?"** |
| Tương ớt / nước chấm | "Do you have chili?" | **"Do you have any hot sauce?"** / **"Could I get some soy sauce on the side?"** |
| Hộp mang về | "I want pack." / "Give me box." | **"Could I get a box?"** / **"Could I get this packed up, please?"** |
| Dao dĩa thêm | "I need fork." | **"Could we get an extra set of utensils?"** |
| Chia đĩa | "Can share?" | **"Could we get a side plate for sharing?"** / **"We're sharing — could you split it for us?"** |

### 4.6 Dessert and the end of the meal

The server's end-of-meal sequence:
1. **"Can I get you anything else?"** / **"Would you like to see the dessert menu?"**
   → Decline: **"No thanks, I think we're done."** / **"Just the bill, please."**
2. **"How was everything?"** → **"It was great, thank you!"** / **"Really good — we
   loved the pasta."**
3. Splitting and paying (→ §5 below).

- EN✗ "We finish. You give bill." → EN✓ **"I think we're ready for the bill,
  please."** / **"Check, please."** (US casual) / **"Could we have the bill?"** (UK)

## 5. Paying and billing in depth (`thanh toán`)

### 5.1 Reading the itemized receipt

English restaurant receipts itemize every dish, show tax, and often separate
a service charge from the subtotal. Learners who don't know the vocabulary may
overpay, be confused about whether to tip, or miss a billing error.

| Line on the receipt | Meaning | VN gloss |
|---|---|---|
| `Subtotal` | total before tax + service | tổng trước thuế |
| `Tax` / `GST` / `VAT` | government tax on food | thuế nhà nước |
| `Service charge` | automatic fee added by the restaurant (usually 10–18%) | phí dịch vụ nhà hàng |
| `Gratuity` (on the receipt) | same as service charge, or a suggested tip amount | tiền thưởng / tiền tip |
| `Total` | everything combined | tổng cộng |
| `Tip` (on the receipt) | space to add voluntary tip on top | tiền tip thêm |

- Critical: if a **service charge** is already on the bill, you do NOT need to add
  another tip. Check the receipt first. **"Is the service charge a tip?"** is a
  perfectly normal question.
- EN✓ **"Excuse me — is there already a service charge on the bill?"**

### 5.2 Splitting the bill — beyond the basics

(Foundation notes in food-cooking.md §8; this deepens the vocabulary.)

| Situation | EN✓ phrase | VN gloss |
|---|---|---|
| Even split | **"Can we split it evenly?"** / **"Split it four ways?"** | chia đều 4 phần |
| Each pays own | **"Can we have separate checks?"** (US) / **"Separate bills?"** (UK/AUS) | trả riêng |
| One pays all | **"I've got this."** / **"It's on me."** / **"I'll cover it."** | tôi trả cho |
| One card per item | **"Can I pay just for the risotto and the two drinks?"** | tôi trả cho phần của tôi |
| One paying, others paying back | **"Put it on one card — we'll sort it out later."** | để một thẻ rồi tính sau |
| Cash + card mix | **"Could we do part cash, part card?"** | tiền mặt + thẻ |

- Teaching note: `"I've got this"` is informal but warm — it means `I'm paying for
  everyone`. More formal: `"Please allow me."` (rare in casual dining) or simply
  `"I'll take care of the bill."` Very VN learners sometimes offer too forcefully or
  use body language only; a clear sentence prevents the table reading it as a
  question.

### 5.3 Disputing a charge

(Foundation: food-cooking.md §7. This note focuses specifically on billing errors,
not food errors.)

| Situation | EN✗ | EN✓ |
|---|---|---|
| Sai tên món | "This wrong." | **"I think there might be a mistake on the bill — I didn't have the steak."** |
| Tính hai lần | "You charge two time." | **"It looks like I've been charged twice for this item."** |
| Không biết phí này là gì | "What is this?" | **"Could you explain this charge for me?"** |
| Giá không đúng | "Price wrong." | **"The menu said $18 for this, but I see $22 on the bill."** |

- The structure: **"I think there might be…"** + factual description. No blame,
  no raised voice — just a clear description. Servers expect this and fix it quickly.
- Following up if it's not resolved: **"Could you double-check that for me? I
  want to make sure the total is correct."**

### 5.4 Paying: card machine interactions

In North American and UK/AUS restaurants, the card machine often comes to the table.
VN learners may not know what the prompts mean:

| Prompt | Meaning | EN response |
|---|---|---|
| `Cash or card?` | payment method | **"Card, please."** / **"Cash."** |
| `Credit or debit?` | card type | **"Credit."** / **"Debit."** |
| `Add a tip?` / `Tip amount?` | optional tip on top of total | select a %, type amount, or `No tip` |
| `Confirm amount: $XX.XX?` | verifying the total | tap / press **OK** |
| `Receipt?` | paper receipt | **"Yes, please."** / **"No thanks."** |

- Teaching note: if you want a receipt, say so before the machine processes — many
  machines don't let you go back. **"Could I get a receipt?"** before or after the
  payment step is always fine.

## 6. Expressing food preference in conversation

### 6.1 `Tôi thích / Tôi không thích` → preference framing

`Tôi thích` = I like (neutral positive in VN). In English, there is a register range
from casual to diplomatic depending on the social context:

| Strength | EN frame | VN equivalent | When to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enthusiastic | **"I love [food]."** | thích lắm / mê | close friends, casual |
| Neutral positive | **"I enjoy [food]."** / **"I'm a big fan of [food]."** | thích | general |
| Mild preference | **"I tend to like [food]."** / **"[food] is one of my favourites."** | khá thích | dinner conversation |
| Diplomatic decline | **"It's not really my thing."** | không thích lắm | when the host is watching |
| Hard decline | **"I don't eat [food]."** / **"I can't eat [food]."** | không ăn được | allergy/strict preference |
| Polite refusal | **"I'd rather not, thank you."** | xin không | when offered a dish you don't want |

- VN: *Em không thích vị đắng.* → EN✗ "I not like bitter." → EN✓ **"I don't really
  like bitter flavours."** / **"I'm not big on bitterness."**
- VN: *Anh có thể không cho cà ri không, em ăn không quen.* → EN✗ "I not used to
  eat curry." → EN✓ **"I'm not really used to curry — could I get it without?"** /
  **"Curry's not my thing — is there a way to leave it out?"**

### 6.2 Describing what you usually eat

At a new restaurant or when a host asks about your tastes, being able to describe your
eating habits fluently is a social skill as much as a language skill:

- EN✓ **"I eat pretty much everything — I just can't do too much spice."**
- EN✓ **"I'm not a picky eater, but I tend to prefer lighter dishes."**
- EN✓ **"I eat everything except pork — religious reasons."**
- EN✓ **"I'm a big fan of umami flavours — soy sauce, mushrooms, that kind of thing."**
- Teaching note: **"I'm not a picky eater"** = `tôi không kén ăn` — a very common
  self-descriptor that puts the host at ease immediately.

### 6.3 Complimenting food — three levels

VN dinners involve active verbal appreciation of food. English has a similar norm,
especially when someone has cooked for you:

| Level | EN✓ phrase | When |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | **"This is delicious."** / **"This is really good."** | any meal |
| Specific | **"The sauce is amazing."** / **"The texture is perfect."** | dinner party |
| Warm + specific | **"I love how tender the chicken is — what did you marinate it in?"** | host's home |

- EN✗ "This good eat." → EN✓ **"This is really tasty — I love it."**
- Teaching note: asking `"What's in it?"` or `"How did you make this?"` after a
  compliment is culturally a second compliment in English — it signals genuine
  curiosity and extends the warmth.

## 7. Register and politeness at restaurants

### 7.1 Getting a server's attention

VN dining culture accepts calling loudly or using a hand gesture to get service.
English dining culture has different norms depending on venue:

| Context | Method | EN✓ |
|---|---|---|
| Casual café / diner | eye contact + slight raise of hand | `"Excuse me!"` if no eye contact |
| Mid-range restaurant | eye contact | `"Excuse me"` softly when the server passes |
| Fine dining | wait for the server to come | eye contact only; a nod is enough |
| All contexts — what NOT to do | snapping fingers, calling `"Hey!"`, waving repeatedly | avoid — reads as rude in EN service culture |

- Opening: **"Excuse me — when you have a moment?"** (polite, signals it's not urgent)
- EN✗ "Anh ơi!" (direct calque) — no EN equivalent; `Excuse me` is the universal
  replacement.

### 7.2 The `ạ` marker and its English equivalent

`Ạ` (sentence-final politeness particle) has no single English translation. The
closest approximation is using `please` at the end of a request, or softening the
frame:
- VN: *Cho em xin thêm nước ạ.* → EN✗ "Give me more water please." →
  EN✓ **"Could I have some more water, please?"**
- The extra softness of `ạ` is achieved in English by:
  - Using `could` instead of `can`
  - Adding `please` at the end
  - Using `when you have a chance` for non-urgent requests

### 7.3 How formality reads at different venue types

| Venue | Register cues | What to adjust |
|---|---|---|
| Fast food / stall | short phrases fine | "Two number threes." ✓ — full sentence not needed |
| Café counter | casual–friendly | `"Can I get…"` over `"I'd like…"` |
| Casual sit-down | friendly, some softeners | `"Could I get…"` / `"I'll have…"` |
| Fine dining | full sentences, `I'd like` / `may I` | `"May I ask what the tasting menu includes?"` |

- The social rule: **the slower the expected pace of the meal, the warmer and fuller
  the language.** Fast food = fast short language. Fine dining = slow warm sentences.
- Mismatch in either direction is noticeable: using formal full sentences at a food
  truck is odd; using bare nouns at a fine-dining table reads as cold.

### 7.4 "How was your meal?" — the exit question

Staff at sit-down restaurants often ask this as you leave or as they deliver the bill.
- VN✗ (silence / nod) → EN✓ **"It was great, thank you!"** / **"Really lovely —
  we'll definitely be back."**
- This question is not a cue for detailed critique — one warm sentence is the norm.
  If something was wrong, this is NOT the best moment to raise it (too late to fix);
  but if you want to leave feedback: **"Most of it was great — the dessert was a bit
  sweet for me, but overall we really enjoyed it."**

## 9. How to use this in content

- Each row in §8 is a candidate `l1InterferenceNotes` entry for `foodOrdering.ts`,
  `restaurantWorkerEnglish.ts`, or any future café/dining topic file — keep the
  **friendly-context** voice.
- **§2 (khẩu vị) is the highest-ROI new territory** in this file: taste and texture
  vocabulary (`tangy`, `tender`, `mushy`, `silky`) unlocks a whole social register
  that learners currently can't access. A learner who can say `"I prefer tender
  meat — not too chewy"` sounds entirely fluent; one who says `"I like soft"` sounds
  like a beginner. The gap is just five words.
- **§3 (café) is high-frequency for young VN learners**: cafés are social spaces.
  Knowing `flat white`, `oat latte`, `room for milk`, and `for here or to go` turns
  a freezing moment into a smooth one.
- **§5 (billing) prevents real-world money errors**: learners who don't know what
  `service charge` means may tip twice, or tip on an already-tipped bill. The
  vocabulary here is practical and immediate.
- **Three-format combo** per topic: grammar note (VN cause + EN✗ → EN✓) +
  vocabulary note (EN term = VN gloss, e.g., `tender` = `mềm / non, cho thịt`) +
  bilingual mirror sentence (full tone-marked VN ↔ natural EN).
- **Full VN diacritics are load-bearing**: always author VN source with tone marks
  (`khẩu vị`, `ngọt`, `chua`, `mặn`, `béo`, `nhạt`, `cay`, `gọi món`, `thanh toán`,
  `tính tiền`, `chia đều`, `ạ`, `khi anh/chị rảnh`).
- **Service-consequence framing** works well here: "Saying `I'm not a picky eater`
  instead of silence tells your host to stop worrying and start ordering — the whole
  group relaxes" gives the learner a social motivation beyond grammar.
- §7.1 (getting a server's attention) is particularly useful as a pragmatics note:
  **the problem is not grammar — it's knowing that snapping your fingers reads as
  rude in EN service culture**, something no grammar textbook covers.
