Eighth in the series, after `interference-notes-workplace-employment.md`,

This cluster is the highest-frequency layer of conversational English. A learner who
can navigate weather, greetings, and compliments can enter most social situations with
confidence. The interference here is structural and cultural in equal measure: VN has
no dummy-subject "it", no tag-question inversion, and a face-saving norm that treats
deflecting praise as humble rather than awkward. The fixes are mostly short — one
pattern change at a time.

## 1. Why these errors happen — structural facts specific to this domain

Five base facts explain most small-talk errors:

1. **VN has no dummy subject "it".** English uses `it` as a grammatical placeholder
   for weather, time, and distance — `it is raining`, `it is noon`, `it is far`.
   Vietnamese omits this entirely: `Trời mưa` (Sky-rain = It's raining), `Xa lắm`
   (Far very = It's very far). → learners drop "it" or substitute with "today" or
   "outside".

2. **VN copula is optional.** `Trời đẹp` (Sky beautiful) needs no verb. English
   weather statements require `is/are/was`. → "The weather nice today" misses the
   linking verb.

3. **VN uses `trời` (sky/heaven) as the weather subject.** English weather uses dummy
   `it`, not a noun. → learners produce "the sky is hot" or "the sky is raining" —
   grammatically odd in English; the sky is a thing you see, not a temperature you feel.

4. **VN compliment culture deflects rather than accepts.** `Bình thường thôi` (Just
   ordinary) and `Không giỏi lắm đâu` (I'm not that good) are polite in Vietnamese.
   English-speaking norms expect a gracious `Thank you!`. The deflection pattern reads
   as false modesty or an invitation for more praise — not as humility.

5. **VN address uses kinship terms; English uses first names.** `Anh/chị/em/cô/chú`
   are calibrated by age and relationship; English replaces all of these with the
   person's first name in casual contexts, or `Excuse me` with strangers. → learners
   feel rude calling someone by first name, or over-use `Sir/Ma'am` in casual settings.

## 2. Weather talk — the universal English opener

### 2.1 The dummy "it" — the most important single fix

English weather statements require a dummy subject `it`. Vietnamese has no parallel.

| VN source | EN✗ | EN✓ |
|---|---|---|
| Trời mưa. | "Is raining." / "Outside raining." | **"It's raining."** |
| Trời đẹp quá. | "Sky very beautiful." / "Today sky nice." | **"It's beautiful out."** / **"What a lovely day!"** |
| Trời nóng quá. | "Today very hot." / "The sky is hot." | **"It's really hot today."** |
| Trời lạnh. | "Today cold." / "Weather cold." | **"It's cold today."** / **"It's chilly."** |
| Trời흐. | "Sky is cloudy." | **"It's cloudy."** / **"It's overcast."** |
| Hôm nay nắng. | "Today is sunny." ✓ (near-miss) | **"It's sunny today."** (smoother) |

- Teaching rule: whenever a VN learner says `Trời + [adjective]`, the English
  translation is **"It's + [adjective]"** — never use "the sky" for temperature or
  precipitation.
- Near-miss: `"Today is sunny"` is understood but sounds translated; `"It's sunny
  today"` is the natural spoken form.

### 2.2 Copula in weather descriptions

`Hôm nay trời đẹp` has no verb in VN. English needs `is`.

- VN: *Hôm nay trời đẹp nhỉ?* → EN✗ "Today weather nice, right?" →
  EN✓ **"Nice weather today, isn't it?"** / **"Beautiful day, isn't it?"**
- VN: *Thời tiết dạo này xấu quá.* → EN✗ "Weather these days bad." →
  EN✓ **"The weather's been awful lately."**
- VN: *Hôm qua trời mưa cả ngày.* → EN✗ "Yesterday rain all day." →
  EN✓ **"It rained all day yesterday."**

### 2.3 Weather subordinate clauses — the "because it rains" trap

VN weather clauses drop both the subject and the `be` verb after conjunctions.
English requires `it` + inflected form.

| VN pattern | EN✗ | EN✓ |
|---|---|---|
| Vì trời mưa nên ở nhà | "Because rain, I stay home." | **"Because it's raining, I stayed home."** |
| Mặc dù trời lạnh | "Even though cold" | **"Even though it's cold"** |
| Nếu trời nắng | "If sunny" | **"If it's sunny"** |
| Khi trời mưa | "When raining" | **"When it rains"** |

- Pattern: **"It + is/was + [weather description]"** — never drop the subject in a
  dependent clause.
- Past vs. present: `trời mưa` is unmarked for tense; use context to choose
  `it's raining` (now) vs. `it was raining` / `it rained` (past).

### 2.4 Temperature — body sensation vs. weather

Vietnamese `nóng/lạnh` applies to both the weather and your body without distinction.
English splits them.

| VN | EN✗ | EN✓ (weather) | EN✓ (body sensation) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tôi nóng. | "I am hot." (ambiguous) | — | **"I feel hot."** / **"I'm feeling warm."** |
| Tôi lạnh. | "I am cold." ✓ (this one works!) | — | **"I'm cold."** ✓ |
| Trời nóng. | "The sky is hot." | **"It's hot."** | — |
| Nóng nực. | "Very hot and wet." | **"It's muggy."** / **"It's sweltering."** | **"I feel so sticky."** |
| Ẩm ướt. | "Wet." | **"It's humid."** | **"I feel sticky / sweaty."** |

- **"I am hot"** is grammatically fine but carries an unintended second meaning in casual
  English — **"I feel hot"** is safer and clearer.
- **"I'm cold"** transfers perfectly — use it freely.
- Humidity vocabulary: `ẩm ướt` → **muggy** (hot + humid), **humid** (technical),
  **sticky** (body sensation). These are the words learners hear but rarely produce.

### 2.5 Appliances: "open/close" vs. "turn on/turn off"

VN uses `mở` (open) to activate appliances and `tắt` (turn off / close / extinguish) to
deactivate them — the same words used for doors and lights.

| VN phrase | EN✗ | EN✓ |
|---|---|---|
| Mở máy lạnh | "Open the air conditioner." | **"Turn on the air conditioning."** / **"Can you put the AC on?"** |
| Tắt máy lạnh | "Close the air conditioner." | **"Turn off the air conditioning."** |
| Mở quạt | "Open the fan." | **"Turn on the fan."** |
| Tắt đèn | "Close the light." | **"Turn off the light."** |
| Mở đèn | "Open the light." | **"Turn on the light."** |

- Rule of thumb: **"turn on / turn off"** for any switch-operated device; **"open /
  close"** for doors, windows, and containers only.
- Casual alternatives: **"put the AC on"**, **"switch the fan off"** — both natural in
  spoken English.

### 2.6 Weather as small-talk opener — the English social contract

Weather talk is _the_ conventional English conversation opener with strangers or
acquaintances. VN learners may find it trivial and skip it — but in English-speaking
cultures, commenting on weather signals friendliness, not boredom.

| Context | EN✓ openers |
|---|---|
| Walking past a neighbor | **"Beautiful day, isn't it?"** · **"Can you believe this heat?"** |
| At a bus stop in rain | **"What a day!"** · **"Awful weather, isn't it?"** |
| First words after a long absence | **"How have you been? Staying warm?"** |
| Stepping inside from the cold | **"It's freezing out there!"** |

- The opener doesn't need a deep answer — **"It really is!"** or **"I know, right?"**
  closes the exchange perfectly.
- VN: *Hôm nay trời đẹp nhỉ?* → EN✓ **"Nice weather today, isn't it?"** is the direct
  equivalent, and it works.

## 3. Greetings — first meeting and ongoing

### 3.1 "How are you?" — beyond "I'm fine, thank you"

Textbooks teach `I'm fine, thank you, and you?` as the universal reply. Native speakers
rarely use it; it sounds rote after the first few encounters.

| Context | EN✓ casual responses |
|---|---|
| Standard / neutral | **"Pretty good!"** · **"Not bad!"** · **"Doing well, thanks!"** |
| Positive | **"Really well, thanks!"** · **"Can't complain!"** · **"Fantastic, you?"** |
| Tired but fine | **"A bit tired, but good!"** · **"Hanging in there."** |
| Honest/casual | **"Busy, but great!"** · **"Good — just got back from…"** |

- The one rule: **always add a question back** — `How about you?` / `And you?` / `What
  about yourself?` A one-sided answer reads as unfriendly even when the words are kind.
- VN learners often answer only the question and wait. In English, the reciprocal
  question is a social _requirement_, not optional.

### 3.2 The greeting register ladder

VN uses `Xin chào` for almost all formal greetings and kinship terms for familiar ones.
English has a register ladder:

| Register | Form | When |
|---|---|---|
| Very formal | **"Good morning/afternoon/evening."** | client meetings, official contexts |
| Standard | **"Hello!"** · **"Hi!"** | most adult contexts |
| Casual/friendly | **"Hey!"** · **"Hey there!"** | friends, peers, younger coworkers |
| Casual check-in | **"How's it going?"** · **"What's up?"** | familiar friends |
| Very casual | **"Yo!"** · **"Sup?"** | close friends, informal settings |

- Teaching note: `What's up?` is NOT an inquiry about current events — the expected
  reply is **"Not much!"** / **"Just chilling."** / **"Not a lot, you?"** Answering
  with a literal update ("Up? I am standing up.") confuses the exchange.
- VN: `Ăn chưa?` (Have you eaten yet?) is a warm greeting — the English equivalent is
  not a food question; it maps to **"How have you been?"** in register and warmth.

### 3.3 First introductions — name and "nice to meet you"

**Name order:** VN family name is first (`Nguyễn Thị Hoa`). In English introductions,
give name first: **"I'm Hoa"** or **"I'm Hoa Nguyen"** — not "I'm Nguyen Hoa."

| Situation | EN✓ phrase |
|---|---|
| Meeting someone | **"Nice to meet you!"** · **"Great to meet you!"** |
| Parting after first meeting | **"Nice meeting you!"** (past form, on departure) |
| Meeting again | **"Good to see you again!"** · **"Nice to see you!"** |
| Confirming names | **"Sorry, could you remind me of your name?"** · **"I'm sorry, I've forgotten your name."** |

- **"Nice to meet you"** (present infinitive) = at the moment of meeting.
- **"Nice meeting you"** (gerund) = on parting, reflecting back on the encounter.
  Mixing them is understood but sounds slightly off to a native ear.
- VN: *Rất vui được gặp bạn.* → EN✓ **"Really nice to meet you!"** — a direct, warm
  translation that works perfectly.

### 3.4 Address — first names vs. "Sir/Ma'am"

VN kinship address (`anh/chị/em/cô/chú/bác`) is calibrated to age. Learners often:
- **Over-use "Sir/Ma'am"** in casual English contexts (sounds stiff at a coffee counter
  or with a coworker of similar age)
- **Feel rude** calling someone by first name when that person is older

| Context | VN instinct | EN✓ approach |
|---|---|---|
| Addressing a peer coworker | `anh/chị [tên]` | First name: **"Hey, David!"** |
| Addressing a manager (first name norm) | `anh/chị [tên]` | First name: **"Morning, Sarah."** |
| Addressing a stranger (service) | formal kinship | **"Excuse me"** (no name/title needed) |
| Addressing older stranger | `cô/chú/bác` | **"Excuse me, sir/ma'am"** (only in service contexts) |

- Teaching rule: **use first names whenever you know them** — this is the default in
  English workplaces and social settings; it is not rude.
- `Excuse me` replaces all kinship terms for strangers: at a counter, on the street,
  in a hallway.

### 3.5 Parting phrases — beyond "Goodbye"

`Tạm biệt` (formal goodbye) maps onto "Goodbye" — but in casual English, "Goodbye" is
used mainly in formal and written contexts. Learners need the casual alternatives.

| Register | EN✓ parting |
|---|---|
| Casual / standard | **"Bye!"** · **"See you!"** · **"See you later!"** |
| Warm and friendly | **"Take care!"** · **"Have a good one!"** · **"Have a great day!"** |
| When unsure about the next meeting | **"Talk soon!"** · **"Catch you later!"** |
| Ending a work call | **"Thanks, talk soon."** · **"Sounds good — bye for now."** |
| At end of evening together | **"It was so nice to see you!"** · **"We should do this again!"** |

- VN: *Chúc ngủ ngon.* → EN✓ **"Good night!"** / **"Sleep well!"** — both work.
- VN: *Chúc buổi chiều tốt lành.* → EN✓ **"Have a great afternoon!"** — a direct
  functional equivalent.

## 4. Compliments — giving and receiving

### 4.1 The deflection trap — why "No, I'm not good" backfires

VN face culture: deflecting a compliment signals humility (`Bình thường thôi` = "Just
ordinary"). In English conversation, deflecting can read as:
- **False modesty** — the listener suspects you're fishing for more praise
- **Dismissing their judgment** — "you're wrong to compliment me" is rude, even if
  unintended
- **Conversation blocker** — the exchange dies instead of warming up

| VN deflection | EN✗ (transferred) | EN✓ (gracious acceptance) |
|---|---|---|
| Bình thường thôi | "It's nothing, just ordinary." | **"Thank you, that means a lot!"** |
| Không giỏi lắm đâu | "No, I'm not that good." | **"Thank you! I've been working on it."** |
| Em còn phải học nhiều | "I still have much to learn." | **"Thank you! There's always more to learn, right?"** |
| Anh/chị quá khen rồi | "You're too kind." | **"That's so kind of you!"** ✓ (this one transfers!) |

- **"Thank you!"** alone is always correct and never wrong.
- Adding a brief follow-up warms it further: **"Thank you — I've been practicing!"** /
  **"Thank you, that's really encouraging!"**
- **"You're too kind"** actually transfers well — it's a natural, warm English response.

### 4.2 Giving specific vs. vague compliments

`Bạn giỏi quá!` (You're so good!) is warm in Vietnamese. English prefers specific over
vague because specificity signals genuine attention.

| Vague (VN-transferred) | Specific (EN✓) |
|---|---|
| "You're so good!" | **"You explained that really clearly."** |
| "Very beautiful!" | **"I love that color on you."** |
| "Your English is very good!" | **"You have such a natural flow when you speak."** |
| "You work very hard!" | **"The report you turned in was really thorough."** |
| "So talented!" | **"The way you handled that question was impressive."** |

- Teaching note: vague compliments are not _wrong_ — they're accepted and appreciated.
  Specific ones are more memorable. Drill the pattern: **compliment + one specific
  detail**.
- For learners who find specific compliments unnatural, the bridge is: **"I really
  like [specific thing]"** — it's honest and natural without requiring elaborate praise.

### 4.3 Complimenting appearances — calibrating directness

English directness on appearance differs by context and relationship.

| Context | EN✓ (safe, natural) | What to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Coworker's outfit | **"I love your jacket!"** · **"That color looks great on you."** | "You are beautiful." (too intimate) |
| Friend's new haircut | **"Your hair looks amazing!"** · **"I love the new cut!"** | "You cut your hair." (just an observation) |
| Someone's cooking | **"This is absolutely delicious!"** · **"I love what you've done with the spices."** | "Delicious, very delicious." (repetition feels flat) |
| General warmth | **"You look great today!"** | — |

- Vietnamese `Trông bạn đẹp quá!` → EN✓ **"You look great!"** — this transfers cleanly
  and is appropriate in most contexts.
- Reserve **"You're beautiful"** for close relationships; among acquaintances use **"You
  look great"** or focus on a specific element.

### 4.4 Replying to compliments on English ability

Vietnamese learners receive compliments on their English and often freeze or deflect in
ways that close the conversation.

| Compliment received | EN✗ | EN✓ |
|---|---|---|
| "Your English is really good!" | "No, no, I still have many mistakes." | **"Thank you! I've been practicing a lot."** |
| "You speak so naturally!" | "Really? I think I'm still not good." | **"That's so encouraging — thank you!"** |
| "I love your accent!" | "My accent bad." | **"Oh, thank you! I try."** / **"Thank you — I'm still working on it."** |

- Pattern: **accept + brief honest follow-up**. The follow-up doesn't need to be
  self-congratulatory — "I've been working on it" is honest and warm.
- VN: *Cảm ơn, điều đó thật sự khích lệ tôi rất nhiều.* → EN✓ **"Thank you, that's
  really encouraging!"** — a direct translation that sounds perfectly natural.

### 4.5 Compliments in work settings

Professional compliments help with peer relations. Learners may hesitate to give them
for fear of sounding unprofessional.

| Work situation | EN✓ compliment |
|---|---|
| Colleague's presentation | **"That was a really strong presentation."** · **"Great job on the slides."** |
| Manager to report | **"Good work on that — you really delivered."** |
| Peer after helping you | **"I really appreciated your help on that."** · **"That was such a clear explanation."** |
| Welcoming a new team member | **"It's great to have you on the team!"** |

- **"Good job"** is always safe; **"great work"** slightly more enthusiastic; **"you
  knocked it out of the park"** is informal and high-praise — useful to recognize.
- VN: *Bạn làm tốt lắm!* → EN✓ **"Great job!"** / **"You did a fantastic job!"** —
  direct transfer works.

## 5. Tag questions — the friendly check-in

### 5.1 Why VN learners avoid them

VN uses sentence-final particles (`nhỉ/nhé/hả/à/phải không?`) as tag-like softeners.
English tag questions require syntactic inversion + polarity reversal (`isn't it?`,
`haven't they?`) — complex for learners to produce in real time.

| VN tag | Direct EN✗ | EN✓ (tag) | EN✓ (safe shortcut) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trời đẹp nhỉ? | "Weather nice, right?" | **"Nice day, isn't it?"** | **"Nice day, right?"** ✓ |
| Bạn đã ăn chưa nhỉ? | "You eat already, right?" | **"You've eaten, haven't you?"** | **"You've eaten, right?"** ✓ |
| Họ sẽ đến nhỉ? | "They will come, right?" | **"They'll be here, won't they?"** | **"They're coming, right?"** ✓ |
| Trời mưa rồi nhỉ? | "It's raining now, right?" | **"It's raining, isn't it?"** | **"It's raining, right?"** ✓ |

- **Safe shortcut:** ending any statement with **"right?"** is always grammatically
  correct and understood by native speakers. It lacks the nuance of true tag questions
  but works in conversation.
- Teaching priority: drill `right?` first for immediate conversational use; introduce
  `isn't it? / haven't they? / won't they?` gradually as an upgrade.
- Common tag pairs to memorize: `is → isn't it?` · `are → aren't they?` ·
  `will → won't it/they?` · `has → hasn't it?` · `do → don't they?`

## 6. Backchannels — staying in the conversation

### 6.1 What backchannel means and why it matters

VN listeners signal engagement with `ừ / uh-huh / vâng / dạ` — short vocal affirmations.
English uses a wider set, and silence during someone's story can read as
disengagement.

| Function | EN✓ backchannel |
|---|---|
| "I'm listening" | **"Mm-hmm."** · **"Yeah."** · **"Right."** · **"Uh-huh."** |
| "That's interesting" | **"Oh, really?"** · **"No way!"** · **"Wow!"** |
| "I understand" | **"I see."** · **"Got it."** · **"Makes sense."** |
| Agreement | **"Totally."** · **"Exactly!"** · **"Absolutely."** · **"For sure."** |
| Surprise/reaction | **"Seriously?"** · **"Oh wow!"** · **"That's crazy!"** |

- Teaching note: Vietnamese learners may stay silent while listening, which in English
  conversation feels like the listener has checked out. A simple **"Yeah"** or **"Oh
  really?"** at the right moment keeps the conversation alive.
- **"No way!"** means the opposite of its literal meaning — it expresses disbelief or
  delighted surprise (`Thật không?` / `Không thể tin được!`), NOT refusal.

### 6.2 Filler words — bridging silence

VN hesitation sounds (`ừm / à / ờ`) map onto English filler words.

| VN filler | EN✓ equivalent |
|---|---|
| Ừm... / Ờ... | **"Um..."** · **"Uh..."** |
| À... | **"Oh..."** · **"Well..."** |
| Để tôi nghĩ | **"Let me think..."** · **"Hmm, let me see..."** |
| Cái này... | **"So, um..."** · **"Well, you know..."** |

- Filler words are normal in conversational English — they don't signal incompetence.
  Teaching learners to use `Um... let me think` removes the silence that makes them
  look frozen.
- **"You know?"** at the end of a statement checks the listener's understanding — VN
  equivalent: `Bạn hiểu không?` — but `you know?` is softer and friendlier.

## 7. Conversation openings and closings

### 7.1 Opening a conversation with a stranger

VN learners may wait to be spoken to rather than initiate. English-speaking social
norms (especially in service contexts or building communities) reward proactive openers.

| Situation | EN✓ opener |
|---|---|
| Neighbor in the hallway | **"Hey, nice to meet you — I'm Hoa."** · **"Hi, I'm in 4B."** |
| Waiting in a queue | **"Quite a wait today, huh?"** · **"Been here long?"** |
| First day at work | **"Hi, I'm Hoa — I just started today."** |
| Joining a group event | **"Hi, do you mind if I join?"** · **"Mind if I sit here?"** |
| Weather opener | **"Beautiful day, isn't it?"** |

- **"Do you mind if I…?"** is always safe for joining any group or conversation — it
  signals respect for the other person's space.
- VN: *Em xin phép ngồi cùng được không?* → EN✓ **"Mind if I sit here?"** — short,
  polite, and completely natural.

### 7.2 Keeping a conversation going — transition phrases

| Function | EN✓ phrase |
|---|---|
| Changing topic | **"Oh, by the way..."** · **"Actually, speaking of that..."** |
| Returning to a point | **"Going back to what you said..."** |
| Asking for more detail | **"Oh, what happened then?"** · **"And then what?"** |
| Showing interest | **"That sounds really interesting — tell me more."** |
| Acknowledging difference | **"Oh, that's different from where I'm from!"** |

- **"By the way"** (`tiện đây` / `nhân tiện`) is the most useful topic-transition phrase
  in casual English. Model it explicitly — VN learners rarely use it.
- **"Speaking of which..."** is a connector that ties back to the last topic: "Oh,
  speaking of coffee, have you tried that new café on Main Street?" — high social
  value, rarely taught.

### 7.3 Closing a conversation naturally

VN learners sometimes end abruptly (because the topic is done) or over-explain their
reason for leaving (politeness norm). English has a short, warm exit ritual.

| Situation | EN✓ exit |
|---|---|
| Quick chat in the hall | **"Anyway, I'd better run — nice talking to you!"** |
| Ending a longer chat | **"It was so good catching up — let's do this again!"** |
| At a party / event | **"I'll let you mingle — great to see you!"** |
| When genuinely busy | **"I don't want to keep you — have a great day!"** |
| After meeting someone new | **"Nice to meet you — hope to see you around!"** |

- **"I'd better run"** signals you're leaving without sounding dismissive.
- **"Let's do this again"** / **"We should catch up again soon"** closes warmly even
  when you're unsure about the next meeting — it's social warmth, not a firm commitment.
- VN: *Thôi, em xin phép.* → EN✓ **"I'd better get going — it was great talking to
  you."** — the combination of `I'd better get going` + a warm closer lands perfectly.

## 8. Seasons and climate vocabulary

Vietnamese uses a two-season framework (`mùa khô` = dry season, `mùa mưa` = rainy
season) calibrated to a tropical climate. English-speaking countries (especially where
Vietnamese immigrants settle) use four seasons, and the vocabulary frame shifts.

| VN concept | EN✓ equivalent / extension |
|---|---|
| Mùa khô | **dry season** (tropical context) · **summer** / **fall** (temperate) |
| Mùa mưa | **rainy season** (tropical) · **spring** / **fall** (temperate, when rain comes) |
| Trời trở lạnh | **"It's getting cold."** · **"Fall is setting in."** · **"Winter is coming."** |
| Đầu mùa hè | **"It's the start of summer."** · **"Summer's here."** |
| Thời tiết thay đổi | **"The weather is changing."** · **"We're getting some mixed weather."** |
| Trời se lạnh | **"It's a bit chilly."** · **"There's a chill in the air."** |

- `Chilly` = mildly cold (not freezing) — an important word for conversations in
  temperate climates where VN learners have no pre-loaded term.
- `"Crisp"` = pleasantly cool and clear (often used in fall/autumn) — a word to
  recognize when someone says "crisp morning."
- `"The weather is gorgeous"` = `Thời tiết rất đẹp` — one of the strongest positive
  weather comments; learners who know it immediately elevate their register.

## 9. Register — casual, standard, and formal

### 9.1 Casual vs. standard greetings — why it matters

VN learners often shift between over-formal and over-casual because the VN register
system runs on kinship (not on lexical variation). English register is lexical:

| Register | EN |
|---|---|
| Very formal (written, official) | "Good morning. How do you do?" · "It is a pleasure to make your acquaintance." |
| Standard (most adult situations) | "Hi!" · "Hello!" · "How are you?" · "Nice to meet you." |
| Casual (friends, peers, young adults) | "Hey!" · "What's up?" · "How's it going?" |
| Very casual (close friends) | "Yo!" · "Hey you!" · "Sup?" |

- Most learners need to live in **standard** and **casual** — these two registers cover
  95% of adult social life.
- Teaching moment: when in doubt, **standard** is always safe. Formal is never wrong,
  just sometimes stiff.

### 9.2 "What's up?" — a phrase learners misread

`What's up?` looks like a question about current events or physical direction. It is
neither. It is a casual greeting equivalent to "How are you?" in friendly register.

| Prompt | EN✗ (literal) | EN✓ (social response) |
|---|---|---|
| "What's up?" | "I am standing up." / "The ceiling." | **"Not much!"** · **"Just got here."** · **"Pretty good, you?"** |
| "What are you up to?" | "I am up to nothing." | **"Just hanging out."** · **"Working on a project — you?"** |
| "How's it going?" | "It is going well in traffic." | **"Pretty good!"** · **"Not bad!"** |

- Drill rule: **"What's up?"** → always answer **"Not much!"** until the student builds
  the social reflex, then teach variations.

## 11. How to use this in content

- Rows are candidate `l1InterferenceNotes` for any social, greeting, or weather-adjacent
  topic — keep the **friendly-context** voice (name the pattern, warm target, never
  "this is wrong").
- **Highest priority for fluency lift:** §2.1 dummy "it" (the single most universal fix
  in weather talk) and §3.1 reciprocal question (learners who add "How about you?" after
  every answer immediately feel more natural to English speakers).
- **Highest priority for confidence:** §4.1 compliment acceptance — learners who can
  say "Thank you, that means a lot!" instead of "No, no, I'm not good" unlock dozens
  of social moments that currently make them feel awkward.
- **Biggest surprise for learners:** §3.2 "What's up?" and its expected response — this
  is a common freeze moment; one drill session removes it.
- **Cultural bridge:** §4.2 specific vs. vague compliments — once learners notice native
  speakers always add a detail, they start listening for it and naturally begin producing
  it themselves.
- Three-format combo per topic: grammar note + vocabulary note (EN = VN gloss) +
  bilingual mirror sentence (full tone-marked VN ↔ natural EN).
- Always author VN with full diacritics (`trời mưa`, `ẩm ướt`, `nhỉ/nhé`, `tạm biệt`,
  `bình thường thôi`, `cảm ơn`, `rất vui được gặp bạn`) — the learner's anchor.
- §6 (backchannels) is an easy win for fluency: learners who already have the grammar
  but stay silent mid-conversation. A five-minute drill on "Yeah / I see / Oh, really?"
  changes how native speakers perceive them.
