once: **parents** who communicate with teachers and office staff, **students** using
academic English, and **workers** (tutors, teaching assistants) who need both registers
at the same time. Vietnamese school culture carries a deep respect hierarchy that shapes
every interaction with teachers — and that hierarchy has no clean English equivalent.

## 1. Why these errors happen — structural facts specific to this domain

The five base facts from the Workplace writeup (no articles, no inflection, no
auxiliaries, topic-prominent, rank address) all apply here. Four more patterns are
especially loud in school and education language:

1. **VN addresses people by role, not name.** `Cô` (female teacher), `Thầy` (male
   teacher), `bạn` (peer), `anh/chị/em` (relative-age markers) carry the whole address
   system. English uses titles + last name (`Ms. Nguyen`) in formal settings, first name
   in casual ones, and "Excuse me" or "Sir/Ma'am" for strangers. → learners default to
   the bare word `Teacher` as a form of address, which sounds like a label, not a
   greeting.

2. **VN school vocabulary is opaque to direct translation.** Each Vietnamese school
   term (`bài tập về nhà`, `điểm số`, `học kỳ`, `tốt nghiệp`) has a false-friend
   calque that sounds plausible but is not the English term. → learners say "home
   exercise", "study point", "study period" where English has `homework`, `grade`,
   `semester`.

3. **VN parent culture is hierarchical and deferential toward schools.** In Vietnam,
   teachers hold near-unquestioned authority; parents thank teachers and apologize
   frequently. English-speaking schools expect parents to ask direct questions, name
   concerns, and even push back — behaviours that can feel rude in a VN frame. → learners
   stay silent when they should speak, or over-apologize when brevity is enough.

4. **"Homework" and academic tasks are grammatically uncountable in English.** `bài tập`
   (exercise/task) is an ordinary countable noun in VN. English `homework` is a mass
   noun; `assignment` is the countable equivalent. → learners say "three homeworks",
   "do the homework exercise", or mix the two terms unpredictably.

## 2. Teacher address: from Cô/Thầy to English forms

### 2.1 The `Teacher` as direct address
VN opens any sentence to a teacher with `Cô` or `Thầy` because the role-word is a
proper form of address.
- VN: *Thưa Thầy, em có câu hỏi.* → EN✗ "Teacher, I have a question." →
  EN✓ **"Excuse me, Mr. Park — I have a question."** / **"Mr. Park, could I ask
  something?"**
- The bare word `Teacher` exists in English but reads as a label, not an address; use
  the title + name or a softener like `Excuse me` instead.

### 2.2 Title conventions
- **`Ms.`** — married or unmarried woman (the safe default for female teachers today).
- **`Mr.`** — man.
- **`Mrs.`** — married woman (still used in some schools; avoid if unsure — `Ms.` is
  never wrong).
- **`Dr.`** — if the teacher has a doctorate; prefer this over `Mr./Ms.` when known.
- First name is normal in many primary schools and some universities: "Hi, Sarah" is not
  rude once the teacher has introduced themselves by first name.
- In a parent-teacher context: **"Ms. Tran"** until told otherwise. In English, using a
  teacher's name warmly is **respectful**, not presumptuous.

### 2.3 Opening a chat with a teacher
VN: *Thưa cô, em muốn hỏi về bài tập.* → EN✗ "Teacher, I want ask about homework." →
EN✓ **"Excuse me, Ms. Tran — do you have a quick moment to talk about the homework?"**

Two moves English needs that VN bundles into the address particle:
1. A softener: "Excuse me", "Sorry to interrupt" (for in-person); "Hi Ms. Tran,"
   (for email).
2. A time-check: "Do you have a minute?" — checks the teacher's availability, which VN
   politeness embeds in the rank-address particle.

## 3. School vocabulary: terms and their false-friend calques

### 3.1 Core vocabulary table

| VN term | Literal calque (EN✗) | Correct EN term |
|---|---|---|
| bài tập về nhà | "home exercise" | **homework** (mass noun) |
| bài tập (at school) | "exercise / work" | **assignment** (count noun) |
| điểm số | "point number" | **grade** / **mark** / **score** |
| điểm trung bình | "average point" | **average grade** / **GPA** |
| học kỳ | "study period" | **semester** (US) / **term** (UK/AU) |
| năm học | "study year" | **school year** / **academic year** |
| lớp học | "study room" | **classroom** (the room); **class** (the group) |
| tiết học | "study section" | **class period** / **lesson** / **period** |
| môn học | "study subject" | **subject** / **course** |
| bài kiểm tra | "test paper" | **quiz** (short) / **test** (longer) |
| bài thi | "exam paper" | **exam** / **examination** |
| đăng ký môn | "register subject" | **enroll in** / **sign up for** a course |
| tốt nghiệp | "graduate complete" | **graduate** (verb); **graduation** (noun) |
| trượt môn | "slip subject" | **fail the class** / **fail the course** |
| học lại | "study again" | **retake** / **repeat** the class |
| nghỉ hè | "rest summer" | **summer break** / **summer vacation** |
| nghỉ đông | "rest winter" | **winter break** |
| phụ huynh | "parent guardian" | **parent** / **guardian** |
| buổi họp phụ huynh | "parent meeting" | **parent-teacher conference** (US) / **parents' evening** (UK) |
| thời khóa biểu | "time subject table" | **timetable** / **schedule** / **class schedule** |

### 3.2 Homework is uncountable
`Homework` has no plural in English. Any specific piece is an `assignment`.
- VN: *Ba bài tập về nhà rất nhiều.* → EN✗ "Three homeworks is a lot." →
  EN✓ **"Three assignments is a lot."** / **"That's a lot of homework."**
- VN: *Nộp bài tập chưa?* → EN✗ "Did you hand in homework?" →
  EN✓ **"Did you hand in the homework?"** (with definite article when a specific piece is
  meant) / **"Did you turn in your assignment?"**

### 3.3 Grade vs mark vs score
All three can describe an academic result; they differ by context:
- **Grade** — most common in US schools; refers to both a single result (`a grade on
  the test`) and letter-grades (`an A`, `a B`).
- **Mark** — more common in UK/AU; same usage.
- **Score** — the raw number (`a score of 85`); used freely alongside `grade` and
  `mark`.
- VN: *Em được bao nhiêu điểm?* → EN✓ **"What grade did she get?"** /
  **"What score did he get on the test?"** / **"How did she do?"**

## 4. Verb patterns in academic talk

### 4.1 Studying a subject: `take`, `have`, `do`, `study`
VN: `học + subject` (study math, study English) maps to several English patterns:
- **`take`** — enroll in and attend: **"She takes math on Tuesdays."** /
  **"I took a writing class last semester."**
- **`have`** — for a scheduled period: **"She has math now."** /
  **"We have gym on Fridays."** (timetabled fact)
- **`do`** — for working on a subject: **"She's doing her homework."** /
  **"I did well in math this term."** (performance)
- **`study`** — for deliberate learning: **"I study English every evening."**

VN: *Con học toán lúc 2 giờ.* → EN✗ "My son study math at 2." →
EN✓ **"My son has math at 2."** (scheduled) / **"My son takes math at 2."** (enrolled)

### 4.2 Submitting work
VN: `nộp bài` (submit work) → English has two equivalent phrasal verbs:
- **`hand in`** — most common: **"She handed in her assignment on time."**
- **`turn in`** — US informal equivalent: **"Did you turn in the homework?"**
- **`submit`** — formal/online: **"Submit the essay via the portal."**
- EN✗ "give homework to teacher" → EN✓ **"hand in / turn in / submit the homework"**

### 4.3 Passing and failing
- VN: *qua môn* → EN✓ **"pass the class"** / **"pass the course"** (not "pass the
  subject", though "pass the subject" is understandable — "class" or "course" is the
  natural choice).
- VN: *trượt môn* → EN✓ **"fail the class"** / **"not pass"** / **"get a failing
  grade"** (gentler in a parent-teacher chat: "she's struggling" before "she's failing").
- VN: *học lại* → EN✓ **"retake the course"** / **"repeat the year"**.

### 4.4 Attendance
- VN: *vào lớp* → EN✓ **"go to class"** / **"attend class"**.
- VN: *bỏ học* → EN✓ **"skip class"** (one lesson) / **"drop out"** (leave permanently).
- VN: *vắng học* → EN✓ **"be absent"** / **"miss class"**: **"She was absent on
  Monday."** / **"He missed two classes this week."**

## 5. The homework and assignment lifecycle

### 5.1 Getting an assignment
- VN: *Thầy ra bài tập gì?* → EN✓ **"What homework was assigned?"** /
  **"What's the assignment?"** / **"What did we get for homework?"**

### 5.2 The due date
VN: `hạn nộp` = the deadline.
- EN✓ **"When is it due?"** / **"What's the due date?"** / **"The essay is due
  Friday."**
- EN✗ "I must hand in tomorrow" → EN✓ **"It's due tomorrow."** / **"I have to turn it
  in tomorrow."**

### 5.3 Asking for an extension
- EN✓ **"Could I have an extra day?"** / **"Is it possible to get an extension?"** /
  **"I wasn't able to finish — is there any flexibility on the deadline?"**
- VN learners often avoid this request (face); in English schools it is normal and
  expected to ask once, calmly. Teachers appreciate the honesty.

### 5.4 "Help with" the homework
VN: `giúp bài tập` → EN✗ "help homework" / "help the homework" →
EN✓ **"help with the homework"** / **"help (someone) with their assignment"**.
- Direction marker: `explain it **to** me` — VN *giải thích cho em* drops the `to`
  which English requires.

## 6. Grades, progress, and academic performance

### 6.1 Asking about grades
- VN: *Con được điểm bao nhiêu?* → EN✓ **"What grade did she get?"** /
  **"How did he do on the test?"** / **"What was her score?"**
- Indirect: **"How is she doing in math?"** is the warmest opener in a parent-teacher
  chat — it invites the teacher to volunteer information before you ask for a number.

### 6.2 "Doing well" vs "doing badly"
VN: *học giỏi / học kém* → EN✓ **"doing well"** / **"struggling"** (preferred over
"doing badly" in school contexts — "struggling" is honest without being harsh).
- EN✗ "study good" → EN✓ **"do well"** / **"she's doing well in English"**.
- EN✗ "study bad" → EN✓ **"she's struggling with math"** / **"she's having a hard
  time with writing"**.

### 6.3 Behind and ahead
English uses spatial metaphors for academic progress that VN does not have:
- **"She's a bit behind in reading."** = below expected level.
- **"He's ahead of his class in math."** = above expected level.
- **"She's caught up now."** = reached the expected level after being behind.
- VN: *Con bị chậm hơn so với các bạn.* → EN✓ **"She's a little behind her classmates."**

### 6.4 Grade letter system (US context)
VN learners may not know the US letter-grade system:
- A = 90–100% (excellent), B = 80–89% (good), C = 70–79% (average), D = 60–69%
  (below average, but passing), F = below 60% (fail).
- UK/AU equivalent: Distinction / Merit / Pass / Fail (varies by institution).
- Useful phrase: **"What does a B mean on her report card?"** — it is completely normal
  to ask this.

## 7. Written school communication

### 7.1 The absence note formula
English absence notes are short, structured, and factual — not the longer formal writing
VN learners may expect.

Fixed opening: **"Please excuse [name] from school on [date]."**
Then: reason in one phrase. Then: optional return date. Then: parent name/signature.

> *Dear Ms. Tran,*
> *Please excuse my son Bao from school on Friday, June 12, due to a family
> appointment. He will return on Monday.*
> *Kind regards,*
> *[Parent name]*

- EN✗ long opening paragraphs with multiple apologies → EN✓ three to four lines; the
  template above is complete and professional.
- VN: `Kính thưa cô` → EN✓ **"Dear Ms. Tran,"** (not "Respectfully dear teacher").

### 7.2 Email subject lines
School emails without a clear subject line are often buried.
- EN✓ **"Subject: Absence — Bao, Grade 3, Friday June 12"** → who, what, when in one
  line.
- VN: learners often leave the subject blank or write a full sentence. A short, specific
  subject gets a faster reply.

### 7.3 Opening and closing an email
| Purpose | Opening | Closing |
|---|---|---|
| First contact | "Dear Ms. Tran," | "Kind regards, [Name]" |
| Ongoing chat | "Hi Ms. Tran," | "Thanks, [Name]" |
| Formal inquiry | "Dear Ms. Tran," | "Best regards, [Name]" |

- VN: *Kính gửi* → EN✓ **"Dear …"** (formal) — match to register above.
- "Kind regards" or "Best regards" is always safe; "Yours faithfully" is very formal
  (used when you don't know the person's name).

## 8. Parent advocacy: asking and raising concerns

### 8.1 The directness gap
VN parent culture is deferential: wait, don't volunteer criticism, thank the teacher for
any news. English-speaking schools expect parents to engage — raise questions, name
concerns, and ask for specific actions. A parent who only thanks and agrees may seem
disengaged to a teacher who expected dialogue.

Useful advocacy frames:
- **"I want to make sure she's getting the support she needs."** (positions you as a
  caring partner, not a complainer).
- **"I've noticed she's finding X difficult at home — is that showing up in class?"**
- **"What can I do at home to support what you're working on in class?"**

### 8.2 Raising a concern without it sounding like a complaint
VN: *Tôi nghĩ có vấn đề …* (I think there's a problem) → if used directly can sound
like a confrontation.
- EN✓ **"I wanted to share something I've noticed …"** — softer opening.
- EN✓ **"I have a concern I'd like to talk through with you …"** — named but not
  accusatory.
- Fact-first frame (mirrors the money-dispute pattern): **"She came home upset on
  Tuesday — could you help me understand what happened?"** Lead with the observable
  fact, then ask.

### 8.3 Asking for extra help
In Vietnamese school culture, requesting extra support can feel like admitting the child
is inferior. In English schools, asking for help is **proactive parenting**.
- EN✓ **"She's finding fractions hard — are there any resources you'd suggest?"** /
  **"Would she benefit from some extra practice?"** / **"Is there a support program we
  can put her in?"**

### 8.4 Leaving a chat positively
Even if the meeting covered problems, closing on a shared commitment helps:
- **"Thank you — I'll work on this with her at home."**
- **"I appreciate you telling me. Let's keep in touch."**
- VN: *Cảm ơn cô* → EN✓ **"Thank you so much"** works perfectly; brevity is warmth
  here, not coldness.

## 9. Academic study register (exam-prep and university learners)

### 9.1 Formal academic vocabulary
IELTS/TOEFL/VSTEP tasks expect a formal register that VN learners often replace with
conversational speech.

| Conversational (EN✗ in essays) | Academic (EN✓) |
|---|---|
| "I think that …" | **"It can be argued that …"** / **"This suggests that …"** |
| "A lot of people …" | **"Many individuals …"** / **"A significant proportion of …"** |
| "very big" | **"substantial"** / **"considerable"** |
| "very bad" | **"detrimental"** / **"severe"** |
| "show" | **"demonstrate"** / **"indicate"** / **"illustrate"** |
| "good" (general) | **"beneficial"** / **"advantageous"** |
| "bad" (general) | **"detrimental"** / **"adverse"** |
| "use" | **"utilize"** / **"employ"** |
| "get" (receive) | **"obtain"** / **"acquire"** |
| "because" (informal) | **"because"** is fine; **"due to"** / **"as a result of"** for nominal clauses |
| "also" (at sentence start) | **"Furthermore,"** / **"In addition,"** |

- These are not vocabulary swaps to memorise in isolation — they are **register shifts**,
  used when the context is formal (an essay, an exam response, a seminar presentation).
  Everyday spoken English does not need them; forcing formal vocabulary into casual
  speech sounds stiff.

### 9.2 Argument structure: the `bởi vì` → `because / since / as` family
VN: *Bởi vì … nên …* (because … therefore …) is the default causal frame.
- EN academic: **"Since [reason], [consequence]."** / **"[Consequence], because
  [reason]."**
- EN✗ overusing `because` at the start of every causal sentence → vary with `since`,
  `as`, `given that`.
- EN✗ calque of *nên* → EN✓ **"therefore"** / **"consequently"** / **"as a result"**
  (sentence connector, not mid-sentence `so` in formal writing).

### 9.3 Hedging — marking claims as non-absolute
VN academic writing tends to be more assertive (a claim is stated as fact). English
academic writing hedges to show intellectual humility and avoid over-claiming.
- **"This may suggest that …"** / **"It is possible that …"** / **"The data appear to
  indicate …"** — these don't weaken the argument; they are the expected academic voice.
- EN✗ "It is true that everyone must study hard to succeed." →
  EN✓ **"Education is widely seen as essential to individual success, though the
  relationship between effort and outcome is complex."**

### 9.4 Speaking in class and presentations
VN learners often stay silent in seminars because asking a question can feel risky
(face) or because they fear imperfect English will be judged. Useful frames:
- **"I wanted to ask — could you clarify what you meant by …?"**
- **"I'm not sure I understood the connection between X and Y — could you say more?"**
- For presentations: **"I'd like to start by …"** / **"To summarise, …"** /
  **"I'm happy to take questions now."**

## 11. How to use this in content

- Rows above are candidate `l1InterferenceNotes` for school-parent, school-class, and
  academic topics — keep the **friendly-context** voice (name the pattern, give the warm
  target, never "this is wrong").
- **Prioritize §2 (teacher address) and §7 (written notes)** for the parent-school
  topics: these are the first real-world friction points for a parent showing up at an
  English-medium school for the first time.
- **§8 (advocacy)** is the confidence unlock for parent topics: many VN parents stay
  silent when they should speak. A single practised frame — "I wanted to share something
  I've noticed" — opens the channel without sounding confrontational.
- For student/academic topics, **§9.3 (hedging)** and **§4.2 (submit phrasal verbs)**
  are the highest-value items for IELTS/TOEFL preparation.
- Three-format combo per topic: grammar note + vocabulary note (`EN = VN gloss`) +
  bilingual mirror sentence (full tone-marked VN ↔ natural EN).
- Always author VN with full diacritics (`bài tập về nhà`, `điểm số`, `học kỳ`,
  `tốt nghiệp`, `trượt môn`, `nộp bài`, `vắng học`) — the learner's anchor.
